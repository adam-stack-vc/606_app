From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Compensation that a broker receives

**Payment for order flow** (PFOF) is the compensation that a [stockbroker](/wiki/Stockbroker "Stockbroker") receives from a [market maker](/wiki/Market_maker "Market maker") in exchange for the broker routing its clients' trades to that market maker.[[1]](#cite_note-basics-1) The market maker profits from the [bid-ask spread](/wiki/Bid-ask_spread "Bid-ask spread") and rebates a portion of this profit to the routing broker as PFOF. Another fraction of a penny per share may be routed back to the consumer as [price improvement](/wiki/Price_improvement "Price improvement").[[2]](#cite_note-dumb-2)[[3]](#cite_note-pfof-3)

PFOF is a controversial practice that has been called a "[kickback](/wiki/Kickback_(bribery) "Kickback (bribery)")" by its critics.[[4]](#cite_note-Q&A-4) Policymakers supportive of PFOF and several people in finance who have a favorable view of the practice have defended it for funding new investment apps, low-cost trading, and more efficient execution.[[5]](#cite_note-5)[[6]](#cite_note-6)


Legality and usage
------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Legality and usage")]

### United States

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: United States")]

In the United States, accepting PFOF is allowed only if no other [exchange](/wiki/Exchange_(organized_market) "Exchange (organized market)") is quoting a better price on the [National Market System](/wiki/National_Market_System "National Market System"). The broker must disclose to the client that it accepts PFOF. Transactions must be executed at the *[best execution](/wiki/Best_execution "Best execution")*, which could mean the best price available or the speediest execution available.[[1]](#cite_note-basics-1)

[Market makers](/wiki/Market_maker "Market maker") including [Citadel LLC](/wiki/Citadel_LLC "Citadel LLC"), [Virtu Financial](/wiki/Virtu_Financial "Virtu Financial"), and [Susquehanna International Group](/wiki/Susquehanna_International_Group "Susquehanna International Group") pay PFOF. Brokers in the United States that accept payment for order flow include [Robinhood Markets](/wiki/Robinhood_Markets "Robinhood Markets"), [E-Trade](/wiki/E-Trade "E-Trade"), [Ally Financial](/wiki/Ally_Financial "Ally Financial"), [Webull](/wiki/Webull "Webull"), [TradeStation](/wiki/TradeStation "TradeStation"), [tastytrade](/wiki/Tastytrade "Tastytrade"), and [Charles Schwab Corporation](/wiki/Charles_Schwab_Corporation "Charles Schwab Corporation"), while brokers that do not receive payment for order flow include [Interactive Brokers](/wiki/Interactive_Brokers "Interactive Brokers") (pro accounts that are charged commissions), [Merrill Edge](/wiki/Merrill_Edge "Merrill Edge"), [Fidelity Investments](/wiki/Fidelity_Investments "Fidelity Investments"), and [The Vanguard Group](/wiki/The_Vanguard_Group "The Vanguard Group").[[7]](#cite_note-7)

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/TechCrunch_Disrupt_NY_2016_-_Day_2_%2826902081436%29_%282%29.jpg/250px-TechCrunch_Disrupt_NY_2016_-_Day_2_%2826902081436%29_%282%29.jpg)](/wiki/File:TechCrunch_Disrupt_NY_2016_-_Day_2_(26902081436)_(2).jpg)

Co-founder of [Robinhood Markets](/wiki/Robinhood_Markets "Robinhood Markets") [Vladimir Tenev](/wiki/Vladimir_Tenev "Vladimir Tenev"). His company became known for helping pioneer commission-free trading by relying on PFOF.[[8]](#cite_note-8)[[9]](#cite_note-9)

In 2014, broker-dealer [Robinhood Markets](/wiki/Robinhood_Markets "Robinhood Markets") introduced no-commission retail stock trades funded by payment for order flow.[[10]](#cite_note-10) Other retail brokerages followed, and in 2020, PFOF received by stockbrokers totaled $2.5 billion.[[11]](#cite_note-11) A 2014 investigation by the [United States Senate Homeland Security Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations](/wiki/United_States_Senate_Homeland_Security_Permanent_Subcommittee_on_Investigations "United States Senate Homeland Security Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations"), led by [Carl Levin](/wiki/Carl_Levin "Carl Levin"), conducted hearings focused on the conflicts of interest inherent in PFOF.[[12]](#cite_note-12) At the hearings, an executive for [TD Ameritrade](/wiki/TD_Ameritrade "TD Ameritrade") said that it routes orders to wherever it can get the highest payment.[[13]](#cite_note-13)

In January 2021, after the [GameStop short squeeze](/wiki/GameStop_short_squeeze "GameStop short squeeze"), officials again questioned whether retail traders were getting the best possible prices on their orders.[[14]](#cite_note-free-14) Rather than direct payment through shares, brokers sold their orders en masse to market makers that executed the trades, paving the way for [short squeeze crashes](/wiki/Short_squeeze "Short squeeze") and [meme stock frenzies](/wiki/Meme_stock "Meme stock").[[15]](#cite_note-15)[[16]](#cite_note-16)[[17]](#cite_note-17) Certain platforms, such as [Public.com](/wiki/Public_Holdings,_Inc. "Public Holdings, Inc."), announced that they would abandon PFOF and add Safety Labels to stocks rather than halt trading.[[18]](#cite_note-18)[[19]](#cite_note-19)

#### Disclosure requirements

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Disclosure requirements")]

U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission rule 606(a), implemented in 2001, require all brokerage firms to make publicly available quarterly reports describing their order routing practices.[[20]](#cite_note-20) This report is used to disclose the practices of "payment for order flow". The report provides transparency in this area, allowing investors to understand how their orders are routed and executed, and to identify any potential conflicts of interest. Broker-dealers must disclose the nature of any compensation received in return for routing orders, as well as the overall process they use for order routing decisions. By mandating this disclosure, the reports mandated by 606(a) aim to enhance the integrity of the market and protect investor interests.

The genesis of Rule 606(a) can be traced back to increased complexity in how orders were routed and executed, raising concerns about transparency and fairness, after the increased usage of [electronic trading platforms](/wiki/Electronic_trading_platform "Electronic trading platform"). In response, the SEC introduced Rule 606 (formerly Rule 11Ac1-6[[21]](#cite_note-21)) under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, aiming to address these concerns. The rule has undergone several amendments to keep pace with the evolving market structure, technological advancements, and trading practices.

One of the significant updates to this rule was in 2018, where the SEC adopted amendments to enhance the transparency of order handling practices. These amendments expanded the scope of the original rule, leading to what is currently known as Rule 606(a).

In 2024, the SEC implemented new rules requiring better disclosure from brokers about their execution quality.[[22]](#cite_note-22)

### Canada

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Canada")]

In [Canada](/wiki/Canada "Canada"), PFOF is not allowed on Canadian listed securities,[[2]](#cite_note-dumb-2) so Canadian brokers charge [commissions](/wiki/Commission_(remuneration) "Commission (remuneration)").[[23]](#cite_note-23) However, according to the current [Canadian securities regulations](/wiki/Canadian_securities_regulation "Canadian securities regulation"), brokers can accept PFOF on non-Canadian listed securities.[[24]](#cite_note-24)

### Europe

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Europe")]

PFOF is banned in the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom").[[2]](#cite_note-dumb-2)[[25]](#cite_note-25) It is also banned in the [European Union](/wiki/European_Union "European Union").[[26]](#cite_note-26)

History
-------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: History")]

PFOF dates back to at least 1984 as noted in the 1993 remarks of Richard Y. Roberts, Commissioner, [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](/wiki/U.S._Securities_and_Exchange_Commission "U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission") (SEC), entitled "Payment for Order Flow" in regards to a letter from Richard G. Ketchum, Director, Division of Market Regulation, SEC, to John E. Pinto, Senior Vice President, NASD, dated October 5, 1984:

> When the Commission first became aware of payment for order flow practices in the OTC market in late 1984, the Division of Market Regulation ("Division") wrote to the [National Association of Securities Dealers](/wiki/National_Association_of_Securities_Dealers "National Association of Securities Dealers") ("NASD") to express its concerns and to request that the NASD "consider possible measures to address any problems observed in this area". In the ensuing years, the Commission has requested information from the NASD and the exchanges to determine the extent of payment for order flow practices.[[27]](#cite_note-27)

Analysis
--------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Analysis")]

### Conflicts of interest

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Conflicts of interest")]

Payment for order flow has been described as a [conflict of interest](/wiki/Conflict_of_interest "Conflict of interest").[[28]](#cite_note-28) PFOF can cause executable orders not to get executed as they are routed to market makers that pay the highest amount. Retail traders generally have less information than institutions ([adverse selection](/wiki/Adverse_selection "Adverse selection")).[[29]](#cite_note-29)

### Lower commissions and fees, price improvement

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Lower commissions and fees, price improvement")]

Since retail orders have a lower chance of [adverse selection](/wiki/Adverse_selection "Adverse selection") for the market maker, they are more profitable for the market maker. These savings are passed on in part to the broker as PFOF, but also to the retail customer as [price improvement](/wiki/Price_improvement "Price improvement"): market makers often fill retail orders at a better price than the best price available on public exchanges. The additional revenue for brokers allows them to charge minimal or no [commissions](/wiki/Commission_(remuneration) "Commission (remuneration)").[[30]](#cite_note-30) PFOF was a key factor in elimination of most brokerage commissions in the United States.[[31]](#cite_note-31)

### Increase in market liquidity and competition

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Increase in market liquidity and competition")]

Several favorable views about PFOF have claimed that PFOF increases [market liquidity](/wiki/Market_liquidity "Market liquidity") and thus reduces the [bid–ask spread](/wiki/Bid%E2%80%93ask_spread "Bid–ask spread").[[14]](#cite_note-free-14) [Bernard Madoff](/wiki/Bernard_Madoff "Bernard Madoff"), an automated stock trading pioneer and later convicted fraudster, was a staunch supporter of PFOF and claimed that by routing orders away from the [New York Stock Exchange](/wiki/New_York_Stock_Exchange "New York Stock Exchange"), PFOF increased competition.[[32]](#cite_note-32)

### Academic research

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Payment_for_order_flow&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Academic research")]

Research published in August 2022 by Christopher Schwarz of the [University of California at Irvine](/wiki/University_of_California_at_Irvine "University of California at Irvine"), Brad Barber of the [University of California, Davis](/wiki/University_of_California,_Davis "University of California, Davis") and Terence Odean of the [University of California, Berkeley](/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") argued that this practice does not appear to affect price execution in a detrimental way for the retail customer.[[33]](#cite_note-33)[[34]](#cite_note-34) According to the study, the market makers give better pricing on a particular stock than the figures quoted on stock exchanges and by doing so retail traders get slightly more per share when selling, and pay slightly less when buying.[[35]](#cite_note-35)
