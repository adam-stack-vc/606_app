Sept 4 (Reuters) - You have to give credit to plaintiffs' lawyers from Levi & Korsinsky: After a U.S. appeals court roundly rejected their original theory in a securities class action against TD Ameritrade in 2021, Levi & Korsinsky came up with a completely different theory to allow customers to band together.
The case originally alleged that TD Ameritrade customers paid higher trading prices than they should have because instead of seeking the best possible price for its customers, the brokerage routed orders to exchanges and market makers that had paid TD Ameritrade to execute its customers' trades. (That practice is known as payment for order flow.)

The 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals trashed the original theory in a 2021 [decision, opens new tab](https://tmsnrt.rs/47dtFgh) decertifying a class of TD Ameritrade customers who traded through the brokerage between 2011 and 2014.
Securities fraud claims, the 8th Circuit said, require proof that plaintiffs lost money as a result of the alleged fraud. Levi & Korsinsky argued that its expert economist had created an algorithm to provide that proof across a class of TD Ameritrade customers. But the 8th Circuit said it would be impossible to establish that TD Ameritrade customers paid higher prices than they should have without examining the particulars of every trade. Those individual inquiries, the appeals court said, would defeat the whole purpose of the proposed class action.

On remand to the trial court in Omaha, Levi & Korsinsky offered an alternative justification to certify the class. TD Ameritrade didn’t tell customers about its payment-for-order-flow deals with certain exchanges and market makers, the firm alleged. So, according to this new theory, when customers paid commissions ranging from $9.99 to $49.99 for their trades, they weren’t getting what they paid for.
All of those customers, Levi & Korsinsky argued, experienced the same loss, paying commissions based on TD Ameritrade’s alleged fraud.
I should pause here to note that TD Ameritrade, now part of Charles Schwab [(SCHW.N), opens new tab](https://www.reuters.com/markets/companies/SCHW.N), has [insisted, opens new tab](https://tmsnrt.rs/4eaWFrn) throughout the 10-year case that it did not deceive its customers and has always sought the best execution of their orders. The brokerage has also argued that it routinely discloses its receipt of payments for order flow in filings to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission.

"TD Ameritrade vigorously disputes the merits of plaintiff's fraud claim and each liability element," the company has said in briefs.
Levi & Korsinsky’s rejiggered argument about fraudulent commissions – a novel theory in payment-for-order-flow litigation against retail brokerages – was good enough for the trial judge, who [certified, opens new tab](https://tmsnrt.rs/3XtXaY3) a retooled TD Ameritrade customer class in 2022.
But the new theory didn’t pass muster at the 8th Circuit. On Monday, the appeals court once again [decertified, opens new tab](https://tmsnrt.rs/4dP9dF8) the class.
Judges Steven Colloton, Ralph Erickson and Jonathan Kobes held that Levi & Korsinsky’s new theory, like its previous attempt, failed to establish classwide economic losses as the 8th Circuit defines the term.
Economic loss, Colloton wrote, means just that: “the difference between the price at which customers' trades were executed and a better price available elsewhere.” The payment of a flat-rate commission, the appeals court said, does not prove that any TD Ameritrade customer would have fared better with any particular trade if they had relied upon a different broker.

The new theory, like the old one, would still require assessment of individual trades, the 8th Circuit said. That's fatal for class certification.
The ruling seems to foreclose any additional attempt to certify a class of TD Ameritrade customers. The 8th Circuit said it would not allow an injunctive class, partly because TD Ameritrade no longer charges commissions, and would not countenance an issue class to determine whether the brokerage breached its duty of best execution because the case would still face too many individualized issues.

TD Ameritrade was represented throughout the case by Sidley Austin. A Schwab spokesperson said via email that the company hopes the 8th Circuit’s new decision will finally end the litigation. “As we have said before, high-quality trade executions and transparency are a cornerstone of our commitment to putting clients first,” the statement said.
Eduard Korsinsky of Levi & Korsinsky said his firm thought the case merited class treatment. “We are considering other options,” he told me.
One possibility might be arbitration before the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, or FINRA. The lead plaintiff in the prospective class action, Roderick Ford, alleged that he placed more than 3,500 trades through TD Ameritrade between 2011 and 2014, so he presumably has a claim worth pursuing, especially because Levi & Korsinsky has said its expert has devised an algorithm to calculate his alleged losses from trades routed to TD Ameritrade’s favored market-makers.
And if Ford’s claims go to arbitration, why not those of other TD Ameritrade customers? As you know, many plaintiffs' firms have become adept at rounding up consumers and employees to assert arbitration demands en masse. Levi & Korsinsky has already sunk 10 years of time and money into the TD Ameritrade litigation, so the firm has good reason to try one last strategy to salvage its investment.
I’m not aware of any FINRA mass arbitration campaign against a brokerage firm, but there’s a first time for everything.
